# Mediterranean Cartography

## Movement Manifesto

Barcelona is best understood not as a city but as a sequence of careful drawings, each plate revealing a single landmark with the precision of an architect's reference book. The Mediterranean Cartography movement proposes that travel imagery be treated like archival cartographic plates: meticulously composed, generously framed, and structurally austere — yet unmistakably warm, because the Mediterranean light is always present in the negative space.

The aesthetic obeys five disciplines. First, every plate honors **a single subject** — a single landmark per slide, never crowded, never collaged. The Sagrada Família does not share the page with Casa Batlló. The page is dedicated. Second, **typography is reverent and sparse**: a serif-ish display weight only when naming the subject, and a pinpoint sans-serif for the data — coordinates, elevation, year, and price, treated as if they were measurements taken in the field by someone holding a sextant rather than a phone. Third, **color is the sky and the earth, never the foreground**: dusk navy carries the structure; ochre and amber appear only as if catching the last six minutes of golden hour against terracotta and weathered stone. Fourth, **geometry is honest**: visible margins, ruled lines as guides rather than decoration, modular grids that an editor would draw with a 0.3mm pen. Fifth, **visual quotation matters**: each plate quotes the silhouette of the subject directly, isolated against the page like a botanical illustration of an iris.

The pieces should feel as if they have been **labored over for hours by someone at the top of an editorial design studio**, the kind that produces small-print books bound in linen. Every line a deliberate decision. Every space a measured pause. The amber pop is not decoration — it is the same light that hits the limestone of Park Güell at 19:30 in late June, and it functions as the thread that binds the five plates into a single carousel.

The text is functional and minimal: a name, a number, a coordinate. The numbers are the price, the date, and the airline — placed with the same gravity as latitude and longitude in an old atlas. The viewer should feel they are paging through the field notes of a meticulous traveler, not a marketing deck. The reference to Canva is acknowledged — the format is recognizable — but the execution is bookish, master-crafted, **the product of painstaking attention to every kerning decision and every shadow value**.

The result, when the carousel is swiped, is a five-plate atlas of a single city, where the deal is announced once with serene confidence on plate one, and plates two through five build a quiet itinerary the viewer can follow.

This is not Instagram-bait. This is editorial respect for the city, for the reader, and for the trade of finding a flight from Munich to Barcelona at 79€ in June 2026. The price is part of the cartography.
